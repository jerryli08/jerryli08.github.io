Morph

Self-folding robot

A 17-DOF robot that folds itself into 150+ shapes from a text message.

Morph is a chain of 17 identical cubes. Each cube is cut in half across its body diagonal, and a servo turns one half against the other in 120 degree steps. You text it, it picks a shape it knows, plans an order of single-joint moves that keeps the cubes out of each other, and folds into that shape on the table while it texts back what it is doing.

We built it in 32 hours at Hack the North 2026. Most of this page is about the software: which shapes this chain can become, how to fold into them, and how to get from any text to one of them.

A robot that is not one shape

Most robots commit to a body: a dog, an arm, a wheeled box. That commitment is also a ceiling. When we were picking what to build, we wanted a robot that did not conform to any one form.

Morph is a single open chain of identical modules. Lying straight it is a line. After a handful of 120 degree turns it is a heart, a letter, an apple. The hardware never changes; the shape is the output.

The hard part was never whether a servo can turn 120 degrees. It was deciding which recognizable drawings this particular chain can physically become, finding an order of moves that gets there without cubes hitting each other or the table, and turning whatever a stranger texts into one of those shapes.

One module, one joint, three positions

Each module is an 80 mm printed cube split into two halves. In our CAD the cut is a plane through the cube's centre, normal to its body diagonal (the line between opposite corners). A Feetech STS3215 servo in the black half turns the green half about that diagonal through a 4:1 reduction.

A 120 degree turn about the body diagonal maps a cube onto itself. We checked it on the real module: after plus or minus 120 degrees the outer surface moves by at most 0.15 mm (tessellation noise), while 60 or 90 degrees move it by 17 to 24 mm. What the turn does move is the face the next module bolts to, so each joint has exactly three positions and the chain always sits on a cubic grid.

Turn it and watch the green half leave the outline mid-turn. At 60 degrees it reaches 64 mm from the centre: 22 mm into each of the three neighbouring cells across its faces, and into no other cell.

There is no plus or minus 240 degree winding: going from -120 to +120 is two steps through zero. The planner, the robot description (URDF) and the hardware driver all share that contract, which is what lets three separate pieces of software agree on what "+1" means.

The rest of the module: 8 mm chamfers around the equator and necked halves, so the 2 mm gap between cubes (2.2 mm measured on the printed spacers) can swing without the halves hitting each other. That gives an 82 mm pitch. From the CAD, the moving half rides on a ring of 40 bearing balls, 4 mm each on a 33 mm radius, and a module weighs about 200 to 250 g.

Seventeen cubes, one path

This is the chain assembled from 17 copies of that one module, mounted with our booth chain's roll word (below). Click or tap a cube to turn its joint one step: everything past it swings as one rigid piece about that cube's diagonal. The example folds are shapes we found by searching this chain's kinematics for this page, not shapes from our booth library.

Every move is checked before it plays: no cube may end in another cube's cell, no cube may sit where the green half reaches mid-turn, and, sampled through the swing, no moving cube may pass through a still one. A blocked move swings partway, stops where the cubes would meet, and swings back.

Kinematics only: the wire-end cube (blue outline) is held still, and there is no table or gravity here. Our real planner also checks the table, the tether and the servo torque, and uses the real CAD instead of plain boxes. Which turn counts as "+" is one consistent convention here; the robot takes its signs from our URDF.

Why most drawings are impossible

The shape is not a sculpted shell. It is the set of grid cells the 17 cubes occupy, each cube sharing a face with the next. A heart is 17 specific cells, visited in an order the chain can actually thread.

The catch is the roll word: the quarter turn at which each module is mounted relative to the one before it, fixed when the chain is assembled. For our 17-cube chain it is 1200130013310123. Each joint can only move its own exit face among three directions, so the roll word decides which paths through the grid are reachable at all. You can see it in the demo: lying flat, each joint can turn the chain left or right, never both, and the mounting decides which.

What we learned about which drawings survive:

  • 1-wide and 3-wide strokes thread; 2-wide strokes mostly do not.
  • Long 45 degree diagonals do not thread.
  • Closed rings need a gap, because 17 is odd: on a square grid a closed loop always has an even number of cells. The Frame example keeps its one-cell gap for this reason.
  • A filled 2 x 2 x 2 block is impossible: a half-swing always reaches 22 mm into a neighbouring cell. The same sweep is why the chain can never assemble or escape a 3 x 3 x 3 cube, even though the kinematics could thread one.
  • The wire end carries the servo bus and power. Its cable leaves through a rigid keep-out (an 82 x 40 mm box in the planner) that nothing may sweep through or crush into the table.

That is the pitch made mechanical: one body, many forms, but only the forms the geometry permits.

Finding shapes worth folding

Recognizability is decided before mechanics, not scored afterwards. The candidates were 17-cell masks, drawn by hand and generated with Claude, several per concept, and only the ones that were both recognizable and foldable made it into the library.

Loading 3D

The gate is fast and the fold search is not, so the order of the funnel matters. Drawn masks also threaded far more often than generated ones: about 39% against about 1.6% in the study we ran on the full 27-module design.

Not every name is a museum-quality drawing. Some are simply the best 17-cell path this roll word can thread.

Planning a fold

A move is one joint, one step (-1 or +1) and a side. Out: the tail swings and the base stays put. In: the base swings and the tail stays put, which reorients the whole finished shape. That is why some letters finish standing on edge.

After every move the pose has to pass hard checks, and the planner searches over signed fold sequences under them.

Loading 3D

The move order matters as much as the goal. Moving every joint at once would drive cubes through each other; the planner's sequence is a collision-free order, and that sequence is what the robot and the simulator replay.

The torque cap comes from the servo: 2.94 N·m bare-motor stall at 12 V, times 4 for the reduction, is about 11.8 N·m at the joint, and the planner caps it at 90% of that, 10.6 N·m. For bench studies we used a more conservative 42 kgf·cm of usable hold.

Text it, it becomes that

At the booth anyone could text a phone number. The message went through Linq's iMessage API to a webhook on our laptop behind a Cloudflare tunnel. The bridge worked out which shape was meant, looked up its planned path, drove the servos and a MuJoCo mirror at the same time, and texted back.

The language path

  1. MiniLM first. A vendored int8 ONNX MiniLM with a small softmax head classifies the text into the closed set of playable labels in about 1 to 2 ms, on the laptop. "Show the judges some love" is a heart.
  2. A fallback onto the same labels. If MiniLM is unsure, GPT-4o-mini maps the message onto the playable label set only. It cannot invent a fold.
  3. Names. "im jerry" folds the sender's initial when that letter is in the library, or a person when it is not. The bot always picks a shape; it never asks you to name one.
  4. Fixed phrases. "open sesame" is a key, and home drives every joint back to zero.

What it texted back

Replies from our test runs:

Folding into a square. 4 moves, about 8s. (deciphered using local MiniLM)

Making a checkmark. 1 move, about 2s (deciphered using local MiniLM)

Going back to a straight line (home).

I worked out the checkmark fold but couldn't start it. Tell my operator.

The last one is the failure path doing its job: the fold was planned but could not start, and the sender got a plain answer instead of silence. When the USB adapter was unplugged, the same plan played in MuJoCo and the reply said the physical robot was not plugged in and that it was playing the fold in simulation instead.

Driving the servos

Every servo shares one serial bus on a CH343 USB adapter. One 120 degree step at the joint is 480 degrees at the servo, 5,461 encoder counts. The driver runs every servo in absolute multi-turn position mode (wheel mode is banned), and a software sign flip maps a logical +480 degrees to the physical direction.

Loading 3D

For each move the executor commands home + state × counts per step × sign, polls the bus until the joint arrives, and can re-trim sag. Home for each joint comes from a software zero taken with the chain lying straight, so a session starts from a known pose. The timing contract is 2 s per step, and the replies quote it: 8 moves, about 16 s.

Simulation

The same plan, twice

Our URDF is the first 17 modules of the full 27-module design: chamfered halves, spacers, diagonal hinges limited to plus or minus 120 degrees, a free base and a floor. A MuJoCo replay plays any exported path with the same timing contract (2 s per step, about 65% speed under load, 10.6 N·m stall), and "in" moves reorient the free base, so standing letters actually stand.

When the bus is dark

Judges could watch the fold in the viewer even when the bus was dark, and a browser fold viewer scrubs every exported path without Python.

What went wrong

Not every run worked. These are the failures we hit and kept on camera:

  • Skipped steps. In early floor runs some joints missed a step; you can hear us call it in the test videos ("is it a skip", "that definitely skipped too").
  • A joint that turned the wrong way. A single-joint test at the wire end turned unexpectedly, and we cut it.
  • Smoke. In one run, as the wire-end cube stood up carrying the tether, one of us says "it's smoking".
  • Moves the robot could not make. When it could not drive home or start a planned fold, the bot texted "Tell my operator" instead of going silent.

Results

  • Top 30 of 1,000+ hackers after 32 hours.
  • A complete text-to-motion loop that ran at a public booth: a local language model, planned folds, every servo on one bus, a live simulation mirror, and plain-English replies, including honest ones when something failed.
  • A shape library where every name was planned, checked, and replayable on the robot and in simulation.

Limits and what is next

  • The roll word, the odd length, the tethered wire end and one-cube-deep occupancy reject most drawings. Morph is not an unlimited shape printer.
  • The full design is 27 modules. The booth robot is its first 17; an 11-cube catalog also exists but is not wired into the text loop.
  • It is not a cloud robot: classification runs on the laptop, and the language model is only a fallback onto a library we exported ahead of time.
