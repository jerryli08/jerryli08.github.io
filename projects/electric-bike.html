Electric Bike

5.3 kW mid-drive conversion

A standard bicycle converted into a 5.3 kW mid-drive e-bike, with every custom part designed by me.

I turned a standard bicycle into a 5.3 kW mid-drive e-bike for my mom, and designed the whole powertrain from scratch. Two 2.6 kW brushless motors share one serpentine belt, a jackshaft carries the drive through a second belt, and a 20-tooth sprocket drives the bike's own chain with 100 N·m of torque.

It does about 35 mph with about 20 miles of range on a 48 V battery and a VESC controller. I did the CAD in about two days and the build on my own: carbon fiber plates cut by an outside shop, bent and powder-coated 5052 aluminum brackets, and spacers and mounts I 3D printed.

How the drivetrain works

Five section views of my CAD, from the motors to the chain. The drive turns as you scroll, every part at the speed its tooth count gives it.

Loading 3D

  1. 1. Two big motors

    Two 2.6 kW brushless outrunners, 5.3 kW together: big, low-KV motors. The cut goes straight across both motors through the middle of the stator. The 12-tooth stator in the middle stays still, and the rotor, a ring of 14 magnets, spins around it and turns the shaft.

    Each motor shaft carries a 16-tooth HTD 5M belt pulley on the other side of the motor mount.

  2. 2. The first belt: a serpentine

    Stage one is 4.5 : 1. Both 16T motor pulleys sit on one 115-tooth belt that drives a 72T pulley.

    The belt path took a good amount of design work. Two backside idlers, each a stack of four 626 bearings, bend the belt into an S between the motors, so it wraps motor 1's pulley 135 degrees and motor 2's 207 degrees. A plain triangle around the same three pulleys would wrap them only 68 and 99 degrees: 3 and 4 teeth in mesh instead of 6 and 9.

    Both motors ride the same belt, so they always turn at exactly the same speed.

  3. 3. The jackshaft: making standards work together

    The 72T pulley shares a 14 mm keyed shaft with a 20T pulley. Everything on both shafts is built around that one shaft size: HTD belt pulleys with 14 mm keyways, 6202 bearings with 14 mm bores, and a 14 mm keyed robotics hub.

    A bicycle chain sprocket (1/2 x 3/32 in) fits none of those, so I designed an adapter plate that bolts it to the hub, with a 1/4 in spacer between them. 3D-printed spacers of 1.6, 2.2, 24.6 and 39.8 mm set where every part sits along the shafts.

  4. 4. The second belt

    Stage two is 3.6 : 1, for 16.2 : 1 overall. An 84-tooth belt runs from the 20T pulley to a second 72T pulley on the output shaft, past a backside idler of six 698 bearings.

    This belt carries more torque than the first. With 100 N·m on the sprocket it pulls about 1,750 N, 3.6 times the first belt. The 20T sprocket on the output shaft drives the bike's own chain.

  5. 5. The whole drive

    Zoomed out, the drive unit fills the front triangle, with the 48 V battery box above it. The carbon fiber plates were cut by an outside shop, the brackets are bent and powder-coated 5052 aluminum, and I 3D printed the spacers and the chain idler mount.

    About 35 mph and about 20 miles of range, with 100 N·m at the sprocket.

    Tooth counts are from the part names in my CAD, checked against the geometry. Wrap angles use the pulleys' pitch circles; belt pulls are torque over the 72T pulley's pitch radius, with no losses.

The build, in depth

The goal

I built this for my mom, and I did the whole design and build myself. It is a mid-drive: the motors sit in the front triangle and drive the bike's own chain, so their torque goes through the rear gears.

Measuring and layout

I started from the stock bike. I measured the frame tubes and clearances with a ruler, calipers and 3D-printed test pieces, then put a photo of the bike into Fusion 360 as a canvas and laid the pulleys out over it. I sketched the chain routing over a photo of the drivetrain: the chain wraps a drive sprocket and runs around jockey wheels. The CAD took about two days.

Motors and controller

The motors are two 2.6 kW brushless outrunners, 5.3 kW together, running on a 48 V battery through a VESC controller. I brought them up on the bench first, ran motor detection in VESC Tool for both, and set up the thumb throttle.

Two belt stages

The drive reduces speed in two belt stages, and the torque multiplies by the same 16.2 on the way to the sprocket, which is how 100 N·m gets there.

Belt 1 runs past two backside idlers, each a stack of four 626 bearings on a shoulder screw. Belt 2 has one backside idler, a stack of six 698 bearings.

The jackshaft and the adapters

The jackshaft needed a lot of attachments to make three standards work together on the same shafts: HTD belt pulleys, metric bearings and a bicycle chain sprocket. I standardized on 14 mm keyed shafts, cut to length. 6202 bearings with 14 mm bores carry each shaft in bearing plates on both sides.

A 14 mm keyed robotics hub goes on the output shaft, and an adapter plate I designed bolts the 20T bicycle sprocket to it; I deburred that plate by hand with a rotary tool. Printed spacers of exact lengths hold every pulley in line with its belt.

Into the bike's own chain

The 20T sprocket drives the bike's existing chain, with a jockey wheel keeping the chain wrapped around it, so the motors' torque goes through the rear gears. The cranks freewheel, so the pedals stay still when the motors drive.

Structure and battery box

I designed all of the custom parts. The carbon fiber plates were cut by an outside shop. The brackets along the seat tube and down tube are laser cut from 5052 aluminum, bent and powder coated. I 3D printed the shaft spacers and the chain idler mount myself. The 48 V battery sits in a box under the top tube, closed with a door and two draw latches.

Two versions of the CAD

Both versions of my CAD, to orbit. Diffing every part between the two files shows that only the chain idler changed.

V1 wraps the chain around the drive sprocket with two 11T jockey wheels on shoulder screws, mounted to the aluminum structure. V2, the one I built, keeps one jockey, moves it 4 mm outboard and carries it on a tall 3D-printed polycarbonate mount that runs up the seat tube. The mounts are lit orange.

What the first rides found

After the first rides I took the drive apart and found three problems.

  • Axial play. In my words from the inspection: "This bearing slips out of here, so I have to add a spacer in here, 'cause there's play in this axis."
  • The second belt needs a tensioner. "I need to add a tensioner in here for the second belt that carries more torque." The numbers agree: at full torque the second belt pulls 3.6 times harder than the first.
  • Damage. A belt stripped a section of its teeth, and a printed spacer next to a 6202 bearing was chewed up.

Results

It rides: about 35 mph and about 20 miles of range, with 100 N·m at the sprocket.

What I would change

The fixes from the inspection come first: a spacer to take the axial play out of the shaft, and a tensioner on the second belt, since it carries 3.6 times the pull of the first.
