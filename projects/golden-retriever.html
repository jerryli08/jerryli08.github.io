Golden Retriever

Assistive fetch robot

Text it what you need and it drives over, grabs it with a learned grasp, and brings it back.

2ndof 250+ teams, 1,500+ hackers
10.5 hBuild time, team of four
20,000Training steps for the ACT grasp policy
6Arm joints, on a lift up a 1 m mast

Golden Retriever is a fetch robot for people with limited leg mobility. You text it what you need; it drives to the shelf on depth estimated from a single camera, finds the item with a side webcam, and picks it up with an SO-101 arm running a policy we trained on our own demonstrations.

Four of us built it in 10.5 hours at the Corgi hackathon in San Francisco and took 2nd of 250+ teams. Below: a simulation you can text, the CAD with every joint rigged, and how each stage works.

Text it, and it goes and gets it

Ask for the pill bottle, the water bottle or the book. The robot plans a path across the room, parks with its arm side to the shelf, raises the arm on its lift, picks the item up and brings it back to you. Ask for something that is not on the shelf and it tells you so.

The replies are the lines our robot sent at the hackathon. The robot is our CAD: the wheels, the winch, the carriage and all six arm joints turn about the axes in that CAD.

Loading 3D

A simulation on our CAD, not a recording. The room is a set of simple props, and the arm plays fixed keyframes here; on the real robot the grasp came from the learned policy.

The problem

Our pitch opened with a number: 15 million people worldwide live with a spinal cord injury. Water, a phone, pills: things most people grab without thinking can mean waiting for someone else to be in the room.

Golden Retriever lets the person ask by text instead. Its replies read like a person talking, not a machine log: "Okay. Going to get the water bottle." and then "Here's the water bottle."

How it works, from a text to a grasp

Five stages, each drawn on the CAD. Scroll to step through them.

Loading 3D

  1. 1. Ask

    A text over iMessage, or the same chat on our web page, names the item. The robot confirms right away: "Okay. Going to get the pill bottle now."

  2. 2. Drive on depth from one camera

    A monocular depth model estimates how far away everything in the forward view is. From that, the robot marks floor cells as free or blocked and plans a path through the free ones.

    Our laptop view drew the same thing: a floor grid over the camera image, green where the floor is clear, red where it is not, and the path on top.

  3. 3. Find it with the side webcam

    A webcam on the side of the robot watches the shelf and runs HSV colour detection, so the item is found by its colour. It looks the same way the arm reaches, and the robot parks with the item right beside the arm.

  4. 4. Grab it with a learned policy

    The SO-101 runs an ACT policy (Action Chunking with Transformers) that we trained on our own teleoperated demonstrations for 20,000 steps. ACT predicts a short chunk of upcoming arm motion at each step instead of a single command.

    The dots ahead of the gripper here sketch that idea; they are not recorded policy output.

  5. 5. Bring it back

    It drives back and says so in the thread: "Here's the pill bottle." If it cannot find the item it says that too, instead of failing silently: "I couldn't find the pill bottle, is it somewhere else?"

The robot

Frame

A box frame of goBILDA low-side U-channel and goRAIL, 349 by 384 mm, with the top deck about 73 cm off the floor. The deck carries the laptop and a clear bin. One corner post is a 1,008 mm channel that runs on above the deck as the mast for the lift.

Drive

Two Axon MAX MK2 servos drive two 72 mm Rhino wheels directly, through 25T low-profile servo hubs, with no gearbox in between. The other two wheels run free on 8 mm REX shafts in flanged bearings. The driven wheels sit 397 mm apart and the axles 322 mm apart. It steers by running the two sides at different speeds.

Lift

The arm does not sit on the frame. It stands on a 136 by 232 mm grid plate on a carriage with two MGN9H blocks riding two 600 mm MGN9 rails on the side of the frame. A continuous-rotation servo at the top of the mast winds a 1 mm synthetic line onto a hub-mount winch pulley with a 112 mm circumference, so one turn of the pulley moves the arm 112 mm. The line runs only from the top, so the winch pulls the carriage up and pays out line to let it down. In the CAD the carriage has about 56 cm of travel.

Arm placement

The SO-101 faces sideways, not forward. The robot drives up alongside the shelf and parks with the item beside it, the arm reaches out to the side, and the side webcam looks the same way.

Built in one night

Asking for things

Requests come in as text. Over iMessage you text the robot like a person; our web page does the same with a chat box that says "Text me what you need".

The robot keeps you posted in the same thread:

  • "Okay. Going to get the pill bottle now." when it starts
  • "Still working on the pill bottle. Looking for the pill bottle." if you text again mid-task
  • "Here's the water bottle." when it delivers
  • "I couldn't find the pill bottle, is it somewhere else?" when it can't find it

The last one is not hypothetical. In one of our takes the robot came back with "I couldn't find the pill bottle, is it somewhere else?" and we asked again.

Getting there: depth from one camera

Navigation runs on a single camera. A monocular depth model estimates how far away everything in the forward view is, and the planner works on the floor in front of the robot.

The laptop on the top deck shows what the planner sees, in four panels: the forward view with a floor grid drawn over it (free cells green, blocked cells red, the planned corridor on top), a clearance map from the depth estimate, a floor mask, and the side camera. The header shows the current drive command, like FORWARD.

Finding the item: the side webcam

A webcam on the side of the robot looks at the shelf while it drives. It runs HSV colour detection, so the item is found by its colour; the pill bottle is bright amber on a black shelf. The target camera panel reads NO BOTTLE until the bottle comes into view, then marks it.

Grabbing it: ACT on an SO-101

The grasp is learned, not scripted. We recorded demonstrations by teleoperation, moving a leader arm by hand over the shelf to reach for the bottle and close on it. We then trained an ACT policy on those recordings for 20,000 steps and ran it on the arm on the robot.

From its folded position the arm lifts, reaches out over the shelf and closes on the bottle.
